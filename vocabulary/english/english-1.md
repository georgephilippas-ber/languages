## soporific

**CEFR:** roughly **C1–C2**.

**Definition:** Causing or tending to cause sleep or drowsiness. Figuratively, it can describe something extremely
boring, monotonous, or dull enough to make someone feel sleepy.

**Grammar:** Adjective: *soporific*. It can also be used as a noun, *a soporific*, referring to a substance or drug that
induces sleep. Common constructions include *a soporific effect* and *a soporific speech*. The word is relatively
formal.

**Example:** *The professor’s soporific lecture had several students struggling to stay awake.* — « Le cours soporifique
du professeur faisait lutter plusieurs étudiants pour rester éveillés. »

**German:** **einschläfernd / ermüdend** · **French:** **soporifique / qui donne envie de dormir**

## unpropitious

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** Unfavorable or unlikely to lead to success; not presenting good conditions or circumstances for
something to happen. It can also describe a moment, situation, or environment that is particularly unsuitable or
disadvantageous.

**Grammar:** Adjective: *unpropitious*. It is the opposite of *propitious* (“favorable, likely to produce a good
result”). Common constructions include *an unpropitious time*, *unpropitious circumstances*, and *conditions
unpropitious for/to something*. The word is **formal and somewhat literary**.

**Example:** *They began their journey under unpropitious circumstances.* — « Ils ont commencé leur voyage dans des
circonstances défavorables. »

**German:** **ungünstig / unvorteilhaft / unheilverkündend** · **French:** **défavorable / peu propice / de mauvais
augure**

## sodden

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** Completely soaked or saturated with liquid, especially water; extremely wet. Figuratively, it can also

**Grammar:** Adjective: *sodden*. Common constructions include *sodden clothes*, *sodden ground*, *sodden with rain*,
and *a sodden landscape*. It is stronger and more descriptive than simply *wet* and is often used in literary or vivid
writing.

**Example:** *After hours of heavy rain, his clothes were sodden and the ground had turned to mud.* — « Après des heures
de forte pluie, ses vêtements étaient trempés et le sol s’était transformé en boue. »

**German:** **durchnässt / triefnass / aufgeweicht** · **French:** **trempé / détrempé / gorgé d’eau**

## squalor

**CEFR:** roughly **C1–C2**.

**Definition:** A state of extreme dirtiness, neglect, and unpleasant living conditions, often associated with poverty

**Grammar:** Uncountable noun: *squalor*. It is most commonly used in the expression *to live in squalor*. Related

## to brook something

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** To tolerate, allow, or put up with something, especially opposition, delay, interference, or
contradiction. It is most commonly encountered in the negative construction *to brook no...*, meaning “to tolerate
no...” or “to allow no...”.

**Grammar:** Regular transitive verb: *brook – brooked – brooked*. **To brook something** takes a direct object and is
not reflexive. *Brooking* is the present participle and gerund. Common constructions include *brook no opposition*,
*brook no delay*, *brook no interference*, and *brook no contradiction*. The verb is literary or formal and is uncommon
in everyday conversation.

**Example:** *She was in no mood for brooking any further delay.* — « Elle n’était pas disposée à tolérer le moindre
retard supplémentaire. »

**German:** **etwas dulden / hinnehmen / zulassen** · **French:** **tolérer / supporter / admettre**

## to scowl at someone/something

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** To look at someone or something with an angry, displeased, or disapproving expression, usually by
drawing the eyebrows together and frowning. It often suggests irritation, hostility, or dissatisfaction.

**Synonym:** **frown at / glare at / look angrily at**

**Grammar:** Regular intransitive verb: *scowl – scowled – scowled*. **To scowl at someone/something** uses **at** for
the target; it is not reflexive. *Scowling* is the present participle. Common constructions include *scowl at someone*,
*scowl angrily*, and *scowl in disapproval*. As a noun, *a scowl* is an angry or disapproving facial expression. Unlike
*glare*, which emphasizes an intense or hostile stare, *scowl* emphasizes the expression of the face.

regard noir aux élèves qui parlaient pendant le cours. »

désapprobation**

## wantonly

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Wantonly** means **deliberately or recklessly, without caring about the harm caused**, especially when
an action is cruel, destructive, or unjustified. In an older, literary sense, it can mean **in a sexually unrestrained
way**; it can also occasionally suggest playful abandon.

**Synonym:** **recklessly** (without considering danger or consequences); **gratuitously** (without a good reason,
especially when causing harm or offence); **maliciously** (with the intention of causing harm); **indiscriminately**
(without distinguishing between targets or considering who may be harmed)

**Grammar:** Adverb formed from the adjective **wanton** + **-ly**. It modifies verbs, especially in combinations such
as **wantonly destroy**, **wantonly kill**, **wantonly disregard**, and **wantonly waste**. It often comes before the
main verb or after an auxiliary: **They wantonly destroyed the building**; **The evidence was wantonly ignored**. The
related noun is **wantonness**.

**Example:** *The soldiers wantonly destroyed homes and crops.* — “Les soldats ont détruit des maisons et des récoltes
sans aucune justification ni souci des conséquences.”

Another example: *The company wantonly disregarded safety regulations, putting its workers at risk.* — “L’entreprise a
bafoué les règles de sécurité sans se soucier des conséquences, mettant ses employés en danger.”

**German:** **mutwillig / ohne Rücksicht auf die Folgen** · **French:** **gratuitement / sans se soucier des
conséquences**

Useful nuance: **Wantonly** is formal and strongly disapproving. It does not simply mean **deliberately**: it emphasizes
an unjustifiable lack of restraint or concern for others. Unlike **maliciously**, it does not always imply a specific
intention to hurt someone; reckless indifference may be enough. The sexual sense is dated and can sound moralizing or
judgmental. Despite its spelling, **wantonly** does not mean **in a way that shows you want something**.

## vaunt something

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Vaunt** means to **praise something proudly or boastfully**, especially to draw attention to its
supposed excellence. It often suggests that the praise is excessive or not fully justified. As a rare, literary noun,
**a vaunt** is a boast.

**Synonym:** **boast about** (talk proudly about something, especially your own achievements or possessions); **tout**
(promote or praise something enthusiastically); **extol** (praise highly, without necessarily implying boastfulness)

**Grammar:** Usually a transitive verb: **vaunt something**, such as **vaunt one’s achievements**. Forms: **vaunts**,
**vaunted**, **vaunting**. The past participle **vaunted** is frequently used as an adjective before a noun, especially
in **much-vaunted**, **widely vaunted**, and **his/her/their vaunted …**. The noun **vaunt** is countable but very
uncommon.

**Example:** *The company never misses an opportunity to vaunt its technological achievements.* — “L’entreprise ne
manque jamais une occasion de vanter ses prouesses technologiques.”

Another example: *The new system failed to deliver its much-vaunted benefits.* — “Le nouveau système n’a pas apporté les
avantages tant vantés.”

**German:** **etwas rühmen / mit etwas prahlen / etwas anpreisen** · **French:** **vanter quelque chose / se vanter de
quelque chose**

Useful nuance: **Vaunt** is formal and uncommon in everyday conversation; **boast about** is usually more natural when
talking about someone showing off. You are more likely to encounter **vaunted** or **much-vaunted** in journalism and
criticism than the base verb. These forms often express skepticism: **their vaunted expertise** suggests that their
expertise has been praised, but the speaker doubts whether it deserves that reputation. Unlike **boast about**,
transitive **vaunt** takes its object directly: **vaunt their success**, not **vaunt about their success**.

## indomitably

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Indomitably** means **in a way that shows determination and cannot be defeated or discouraged**, even
by serious difficulties or opposition.

**Synonym:** **resolutely** (with firm determination); **undauntedly** (without being discouraged or intimidated);
**unshakably** (in a way that remains firm despite pressure)

**Grammar:** Adverb formed from the adjective **indomitable**, with **-ably** replacing **-able**. It can modify a verb,
as in **fight indomitably**, or an adjective, as in **indomitably cheerful**. It has no inflected forms; comparison,
when needed, uses **more indomitably** and **most indomitably**.

**Example:** *Despite repeated setbacks, she remained indomitably optimistic.* — “Malgré des revers répétés, elle est
restée d’un optimisme inébranlable.”

Another example: *They fought indomitably to preserve their language and traditions.* — “Ils ont lutté avec une
détermination inébranlable pour préserver leur langue et leurs traditions.”

**German:** **unbeugsam / unerschütterlich / unbezwingbar** · **French:** **avec une détermination inébranlable / de
manière indomptable**

Useful nuance: **Indomitably** is uncommon and literary or formal, usually expressing admiration. It suggests not merely
persistence but a spirit that hardship cannot break. In **indomitably cheerful** or **indomitably optimistic**, it
describes someone whose positive attitude survives adversity. In everyday English, **without giving up** or **with
unwavering determination** is often more natural.

## equivocal

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Equivocal** means **unclear or open to more than one interpretation**, especially when a statement or
response avoids expressing a definite opinion or commitment. It can also describe evidence, results, or signs that are
**inconclusive** and do not clearly support one conclusion.

**Synonym:** **ambiguous** (having more than one possible meaning); **noncommittal** (not expressing a definite opinion
or intention); **inconclusive** (not providing a clear answer or result)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used before a noun or after a linking verb: **an equivocal answer**, **equivocal evidence**,
**her response was equivocal**. Common combinations include **equivocal about something** and **equivocal on an issue**.
Comparison normally uses **more equivocal** and **most equivocal**. The adverb is **equivocally**; the opposite is
**unequivocal**, meaning clear and definite.

**Example:** *She gave an equivocal answer when asked whether she supported the proposal.* — “Elle a donné une réponse
équivoque lorsqu’on lui a demandé si elle soutenait la proposition.”

Another example: *The test results were equivocal, so further investigation was needed.* — “Les résultats du test
n’étaient pas concluants, et des examens supplémentaires étaient donc nécessaires.”

**German:** **mehrdeutig / ausweichend / nicht eindeutig** · **French:** **équivoque / ambigu / non concluant**

Useful nuance: **Equivocal** is formal. An **equivocal answer** may suggest deliberate evasiveness, but **equivocal
evidence** is simply unclear or inconclusive; it does not imply deception. **Ambivalent** is different: it describes
having mixed or conflicting feelings, not an unclear meaning. **Unequivocal support** means clear, definite support
without reservations.

## demur / demur at something

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Demur** means to **express an objection or reservation**, often politely, or to show reluctance to
agree to something. **Demurred** is the past simple and past participle of **demur**. In legal language, **demur** can
also mean to raise a formal objection to the legal sufficiency of a claim rather than dispute its facts.

**Synonym:** **object** (express disagreement or opposition); **express reservations** (say that you have doubts or
concerns); **balk at** (be unwilling to accept or do something; often stronger and less formal)

**Grammar:** Intransitive verb: **demur – demurred – demurred**; present participle **demurring**. The final **r**
doubles before **-ed** and **-ing**. Use **demur at something**, or use the verb without an object when the context
makes the objection clear: **She demurred at the suggestion**; **He agreed, but she demurred**. The noun **demur**
occurs especially in **without demur**, meaning “without objection.”

**Example:** *When they asked her to work another weekend, she demurred.* — “Quand ils lui ont demandé de travailler un
week-end de plus, elle a émis des réserves.”

Another example: *He demurred at the suggestion that he should pay the entire bill.* — “Il a protesté contre l’idée
qu’il devait payer la totalité de l’addition.”

**German:** **Einwände erheben / Bedenken äußern** · **French:** **émettre des réserves / objecter**

Useful nuance: **Demur** is formal and especially common in written English. It often suggests a measured or polite
objection, but does not necessarily mean a definite refusal. It is not simply a synonym for **hesitate**: someone who
**demurs** signals disagreement or reluctance, rather than merely pausing. Do not confuse **demur** with **demure**, an
adjective meaning quiet, modest, or reserved in manner.

## cower / cower in fear

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Cower** means to lower your body or shrink back because you are frightened, often to protect yourself
or avoid being noticed. It can also mean to behave fearfully or submissively when faced with threats, criticism, or
authority, without necessarily moving your body. **Cowering** is the **-ing** form of **cower**.

**Synonym:** **cringe** (shrink back in fear; also commonly means to feel embarrassed); **shrink back** (move away
because of fear or discomfort); **huddle** (curl up or gather closely together, often for warmth or protection, not
necessarily through fear)

**Grammar:** Intransitive verb: **cower – cowered – cowered**; **-ing** form: **cowering**. It does not take a direct
object. Common patterns include **cower in fear**, **cower behind something**, **cower under something**, and **cower
before someone**. **Cowering** can form a continuous tense, as in **was cowering**, or describe a noun, as in **a
cowering animal**.

**Example:** *The dog was cowering under the table during the thunderstorm.* — “Le chien se recroquevillait sous la
table pendant l’orage.”

Another example: *She refused to cower before anyone who tried to intimidate her.* — “Elle refusait de se laisser
intimider par quiconque essayait de lui faire peur.”

**German:** **sich vor Angst ducken / sich verängstigt zusammenkauern / kuschen** · **French:** **se recroqueviller de
peur / trembler devant quelqu’un**

Useful nuance: **Cowering** suggests visible fear and vulnerability, not simply being nervous. Unlike **crouching**,
which describes a low body position for any reason, **cowering** normally implies fear or submission. Applied to people,
it can sound critical or contemptuous, especially in expressions such as **cower before authority**. **Cowering** and
**cowardly** are related in meaning but are not interchangeable: someone may be **cowering** in immediate danger without
generally being **cowardly**.

## tatty

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Tatty** means **shabby**, **worn**, or **untidy-looking**, especially because something is old, has
been used a lot, or has not been looked after. It commonly describes clothes, furniture, books, and buildings.

**Synonym:** **shabby** (worn and in poor condition); **scruffy** (untidy-looking; also commonly describes people);
**worn-out** (damaged or no longer useful through use; often stronger than **tatty**)

**Grammar:** Adjective, mainly British English and informal. Used before a noun or after a linking verb: **a tatty

**tatty clothes**, **a tatty old sofa**, and **a bit tatty**.

**Example:** *He was wearing a tatty old jumper with holes in the sleeves.* — “Il portait un vieux pull élimé avec des

Another example: *The book is a bit tatty, but all the pages are there.* — “Le livre est un peu abîmé, mais toutes les
pages sont là.”

**German:** **schäbig / abgetragen / ramponiert** · **French:** **défraîchi / élimé / en mauvais état**

Useful nuance: **Tatty** usually concerns appearance and condition, not cleanliness: something can be clean but still
look **tatty**. It often suggests frayed edges, faded fabric, scratches, or general wear rather than serious damage.
Unlike **worn-out**, it does not necessarily mean that something can no longer be used. It can also suggest a cheap or
poorly finished appearance. **Shabby** is a more widely understood alternative outside British English.

## a walkout

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** A **walkout** is an occasion when workers stop working and leave their workplace together as a protest
or strike. It can also mean an occasion when people deliberately leave a meeting, performance, or other event to show
their disapproval.

**Synonym:** **strike** (a collective refusal to work; not necessarily involving leaving the workplace); **protest
departure** (a descriptive phrase for leaving an event to show disapproval)

**Grammar:** Countable noun: **a walkout**; plural **walkouts**. Common combinations include **stage a walkout**,
**organize a walkout**, **a mass walkout**, and **a walkout over pay**. The noun is written as one word; the related
phrasal verb **walk out** is written as two words: **The workers walked out over pay**.

**Example:** *The workers staged a walkout over unsafe working conditions.* — “Les salariés ont organisé un débrayage
pour protester contre des conditions de travail dangereuses.”

Another example: *Several delegates organized a walkout during the minister’s speech.* — “Plusieurs délégués ont
organisé un départ collectif en signe de protestation pendant le discours du ministre.”

**German:** **der Streik / die Arbeitsniederlegung / das demonstrative Verlassen einer Veranstaltung** · **French:**
**le débrayage / la grève / le départ collectif en signe de protestation**

Useful nuance: A **walkout** emphasizes the act of leaving or stopping work as a visible protest; it is often brief, but
it need not be. A **strike** can last much longer and does not necessarily involve a collective departure from the
workplace. Simply leaving an event early is not normally called a **walkout** unless it expresses disapproval. The
related phrase **walk out on someone** means to abandon that person or a relationship, not to take part in a strike.

## languid

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Languid** means **lacking energy** or **moving slowly and without effort**, often because of heat,
tiredness, or a relaxed mood. It can describe a person, their movements, or an atmosphere. It sometimes suggests
physical weakness, but often conveys pleasant, unhurried relaxation.

**Synonym:** **listless** (lacking energy or interest); **lethargic** (unusually tired and inactive; often more
negative); **unhurried** (not rushed; without necessarily implying tiredness); **relaxed** (calm and free from tension)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used before a noun or after a linking verb: **a languid wave**, **languid movements**, **She
felt languid**. Comparative and superlative: **more languid**, **most languid**. The related adverb is **languidly**:
**He stretched languidly**. The related noun **languor** describes a state of pleasant tiredness or lack of energy.

**Example:** *She gave us a languid wave from her deckchair.* — “Elle nous a fait un signe de la main nonchalant depuis
sa chaise longue.”

Another example: *The afternoon heat left him feeling languid and sleepy.* — “La chaleur de l’après-midi le laissait
sans énergie et somnolent.”

**German:** **träge / matt / lässig** · **French:** **languide / nonchalant / sans énergie**

Useful nuance: **Languid** is a relatively formal or literary word. Unlike **lazy**, it does not necessarily criticize
someone’s willingness to work. A **languid gesture** may seem graceful, casually indifferent, or subtly seductive, while
feeling **languid** after an illness suggests weakness. Context determines whether the lack of energy feels pleasant or
unwelcome.
