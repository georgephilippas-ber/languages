## furtive

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** Done in a secretive, cautious, or stealthy way, especially because someone wants to avoid being noticed.
It often describes looks, movements, gestures, or behavior that seem hidden or suspicious.

**Grammar:** Adjective: *furtive*. Common expressions include *a furtive glance*, *a furtive look*, *a furtive
movement*, and *furtive behavior*. Related adverb: *furtively*; related noun: *furtiveness*.

**Example:** *He cast a furtive glance at the door before slipping out of the room.* — « Il jeta un regard furtif vers
la porte avant de se glisser hors de la pièce. »

**German:** **verstohlen / heimlich** · **French:** **furtif / discret / à la dérobée**

## desultory

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** Lacking a clear plan, purpose, or sustained effort; moving irregularly from one thing to another without
much consistency or direction. It can describe conversation, work, reading, attempts, or activity.

**Synonym:** **aimless / unfocused / haphazard**

**Grammar:** Adjective. Common combinations include *desultory conversation*, *desultory efforts*, *desultory reading*,
and *desultory progress*.

**Example:** *After a few desultory attempts to solve the problem, he gave up.*

**German:** **planlos / sprunghaft / halbherzig** · **French:** **décousu / désordonné / sans suite**

## foray

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** A short, often experimental attempt to become involved in a new activity or field. It can also mean a
sudden raid or incursion into another area.

**Synonym:** **venture / incursion / brief attempt**

**Grammar:** Countable noun: *a foray*; plural: *forays*. Common constructions include *a foray into something* and
*make a foray into something*.

**Example:** *The company’s first foray into electric vehicles was surprisingly successful.*

**German:** **Ausflug / Vorstoß / erster Versuch** · **French:** **incursion / tentative / percée**

## inscrutable

**CEFR:** roughly **C1–C2**.

**Definition:** Very difficult or impossible to understand, interpret, or explain, especially when referring to a
person’s expression, motives, behavior, or something complex and obscure.

**Synonym:** **mysterious / enigmatic / unfathomable**

**Grammar:** Adjective. Common combinations include *inscrutable expression*, *inscrutable motives*, and *inscrutable
smile*.

**Example:** *He listened in silence, his expression completely inscrutable.*

**German:** **unergründlich / undurchschaubar / rätselhaft** · **French:** **impénétrable / énigmatique / insondable**

## equivocal

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Equivocal** means **unclear or open to more than one interpretation**, especially when a statement or
response avoids expressing a definite opinion or commitment. It can also describe evidence, results, or signs that are
**inconclusive** and do not clearly support one conclusion.

**Synonym:** **ambiguous** (having more than one possible meaning); **noncommittal** (not expressing a definite opinion
or intention); **inconclusive** (not providing a clear answer or result)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used before a noun or after a linking verb: **an equivocal answer**, **equivocal evidence**,
**her response was equivocal**. Common combinations include **equivocal about something** and **equivocal on an issue**.
Comparison normally uses **more equivocal** and **most equivocal**. The adverb is **equivocally**; the opposite is
**unequivocal**, meaning clear and definite.

**Example:** *She gave an equivocal answer when asked whether she supported the proposal.* — “Elle a donné une réponse
équivoque lorsqu’on lui a demandé si elle soutenait la proposition.”

Another example: *The test results were equivocal, so further investigation was needed.* — “Les résultats du test
n’étaient pas concluants, et des examens supplémentaires étaient donc nécessaires.”

**German:** **mehrdeutig / ausweichend / nicht eindeutig** · **French:** **équivoque / ambigu / non concluant**

Useful nuance: **Equivocal** is formal. An **equivocal answer** may suggest deliberate evasiveness, but **equivocal
evidence** is simply unclear or inconclusive; it does not imply deception. **Ambivalent** is different: it describes
having mixed or conflicting feelings, not an unclear meaning. **Unequivocal support** means clear, definite support
without reservations.

## demur / demur at something

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Demur** means to **express an objection or reservation**, often politely, or to show reluctance to
agree to something. **Demurred** is the past simple and past participle of **demur**. In legal language, **demur** can
also mean to raise a formal objection to the legal sufficiency of a claim rather than dispute its facts.

**Synonym:** **object** (express disagreement or opposition); **express reservations** (say that you have doubts or
concerns); **balk at** (be unwilling to accept or do something; often stronger and less formal)

**Grammar:** Intransitive verb: **demur – demurred – demurred**; present participle **demurring**. The final **r**
doubles before **-ed** and **-ing**. Use **demur at something**, or use the verb without an object when the context
makes the objection clear: **She demurred at the suggestion**; **He agreed, but she demurred**. The noun **demur**
occurs especially in **without demur**, meaning “without objection.”

**Example:** *When they asked her to work another weekend, she demurred.* — “Quand ils lui ont demandé de travailler un
week-end de plus, elle a émis des réserves.”

Another example: *He demurred at the suggestion that he should pay the entire bill.* — “Il a protesté contre l’idée
qu’il devait payer la totalité de l’addition.”

**German:** **Einwände erheben / Bedenken äußern** · **French:** **émettre des réserves / objecter**

Useful nuance: **Demur** is formal and especially common in written English. It often suggests a measured or polite
objection, but does not necessarily mean a definite refusal. It is not simply a synonym for **hesitate**: someone who
**demurs** signals disagreement or reluctance, rather than merely pausing. Do not confuse **demur** with **demure**, an
adjective meaning quiet, modest, or reserved in manner.

## to field something

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **To field something** means to receive and deal with it, especially a **question**, **request**,
**inquiry**, or **complaint**. It often suggests responding competently or promptly, sometimes under pressure. In sport,
it also means to catch or stop a ball, or to select and put a team or player into a game. In politics, an organization
can **field a candidate** by putting them forward for election.

**Synonym:** **handle** (deal with something); **answer** (respond to a question or inquiry); **deal with** (take
responsibility for responding to something)

**Grammar:** Usually a transitive verb: **to field something**. Regular forms: **fields**, **fielded**, **fielding**.
Common combinations include **field questions**, **field calls**, **field inquiries**, and **field complaints**. The
source can be introduced with **from**: **field questions from reporters**. In cricket and baseball, it can also be
intransitive: **Our team is fielding first**.

**Example:** *The spokesperson fielded questions from reporters after the announcement.* — “Le porte-parole a répondu
aux questions des journalistes après l’annonce.”

Another example: *Our support team fields hundreds of calls every day.* — “Notre équipe d’assistance traite des
centaines d’appels chaque jour.”

**German:** **Fragen beantworten / Anfragen oder Beschwerden bearbeiten / eine Mannschaft aufstellen** · **French:**
**répondre à des questions / traiter des demandes ou des réclamations / aligner une équipe**

Useful nuance: **Field** emphasizes receiving and handling what comes your way, rather than merely giving an answer.
**Fielding questions** may involve unexpected or difficult questions, but does not necessarily imply difficulty or a
successful resolution. It is especially common in professional, media, and public-speaking contexts. In sport, **field a
ball** means to catch or stop it and usually return it; **field a team** means to put a team into a match or
competition. The noun **field** commonly means an area of land, a sports ground, or an area of study or work.

## to quash something

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Quash** means to officially cancel a legal decision or order, or to forcefully stop something such as
a rebellion, a rumour, or opposition.

**Synonym:** **overturn** (reverse a legal decision); **suppress** (stop an activity or opposition by force or
authority); **put an end to** (stop something)

**Grammar:** Regular, transitive verb: **quash – quashed – quashed**; third-person singular: **quashes**; present
participle: **quashing**. It takes a direct object without a preposition: **quash a conviction**, **quash a rebellion**,
**quash rumours**. The passive is common in legal contexts: **the conviction was quashed**.

**Example:** *The court quashed his conviction after new evidence emerged.* — “Le tribunal a annulé sa condamnation
après l’apparition de nouveaux éléments de preuve.”

Another example: *The company issued a statement to quash rumours of its closure.* — “L’entreprise a publié un
communiqué pour mettre fin aux rumeurs concernant sa fermeture.”

**German:** **aufheben / niederschlagen / im Keim ersticken** · **French:** **annuler / réprimer / étouffer**

Useful nuance: **Quash** is formal and particularly common in legal and news reporting. In law, it means to invalidate a
conviction, ruling, or order—not merely to criticise it. With **rumours**, **hopes**, or **opposition**, it means to put
a decisive end to them. Do not confuse **quash** with **squash**, which commonly means to crush or flatten something
physically, although **squash** can also be used figuratively for stopping rumours or opposition.

## quibble about/over something / a quibble

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** To **quibble** means to argue or complain about small details, especially details that seem unimportant.
A **quibble** is a minor objection or criticism.

**Synonym:** **nitpick** (criticize tiny details); **split hairs** (make unnecessarily fine distinctions)

**Grammar:** **Quibble** is usually an intransitive verb: **quibbles – quibbled – quibbled**; **quibbling**. Common
patterns are **quibble about/over something** and **quibble with someone about something**. The noun is countable: **a
quibble**, plural **quibbles**.

**Example:** *Let's not quibble over a few euros.* — “Ne chipotons pas pour quelques euros.”

Another example: *My only quibble with the book is that the ending feels rushed.* — “Ma seule petite réserve concernant
ce livre est que la fin semble bâclée.”

**German:** **über Kleinigkeiten streiten / an etwas herummäkeln; der kleine Einwand** · **French:** **chipoter /
pinailler ; la petite réserve / l'objection mineure**

Useful nuance: **Quibble** usually suggests that an objection is too minor to deserve much attention, so the verb often
sounds disapproving. However, **my only quibble** is a common way to soften a small criticism while expressing an
otherwise positive opinion.
