## surly

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Surly** means **bad-tempered and unfriendly**, often showing this by speaking rudely, giving very
short answers, or refusing to be pleasant. It can describe a person, their manner, or their replies and expressions.

**Synonym:** **grumpy** (bad-tempered; more informal and not necessarily rude); **sullen** (silently unhappy or
resentful); **ill-tempered** (easily annoyed or angry); **unfriendly** (not warm or welcoming, but not necessarily
bad-tempered)

**Grammar:** Adjective: **a surly waiter**, **a surly reply**, **He was surly with everyone**. Comparative **surlier**;
superlative **surliest**. Despite its **-ly** ending, **surly** is not an adverb. The corresponding adverb is
**surlily**, though **in a surly manner** is often more natural. The noun is **surliness**.

**Example:** *The surly receptionist barely looked up when we arrived.* — “Le réceptionniste revêche a à peine levé les
yeux à notre arrivée.”

Another example: *He gave a surly reply and turned away.* — “Il a répondu d’un ton bourru et s’est détourné.”

**German:** **mürrisch / unfreundlich / barsch** · **French:** **revêche / bourru / hargneux**

Useful nuance: **Surly** is disapproving and suggests more than simply being quiet or reserved: the person seems
irritated and unwilling to be civil. Someone who is **grumpy** may still be affectionate or approachable, whereas
someone who is **surly** comes across as unpleasant or rude. It can describe either a temporary mood or someone’s usual
manner.

## gambol

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Gambol** means to **run and jump about playfully**, especially as young animals or children do.
**Gambolling** is the British English **-ing** form of **gambol**.

**Synonym:** **frolic** (play or move about happily); **frisk** (move with quick, playful jumps, especially of animals);
**romp** (play energetically)

**Grammar:** Intransitive verb: **gambol**, **gambols**, **gambolled**, **gambolling** in British English; American
English usually has **gamboled**, **gamboling**. Often used with a phrase indicating location or direction: **gambol in
a field**, **gambol across the grass**. **Gambolling** can be a present participle, as in **the lambs were gambolling**,
or modify a noun, as in **gambolling lambs**. **Gambol** is also a countable noun meaning a playful leap or movement,
often used in the plural: **gambols**.

**Example:** *The lambs were gambolling in the spring sunshine.* — “Les agneaux gambadaient sous le soleil printanier.”

Another example: *We watched the puppies gambolling across the lawn.* — “Nous regardions les chiots gambader sur la
pelouse.”

**German:** **herumtollen / umherspringen** · **French:** **gambader / folâtrer**

Useful nuance: **Gambol** is a literary or descriptive word rather than an everyday conversational choice. It suggests
carefree energy and playful jumps, not simply running. It is particularly associated with lambs and other young animals;
used of adults, it can sound humorous. Do not confuse **gambolling** with **gambling**, which means betting money or
taking risks.

## untrammelled

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **Untrammelled** means **not restricted or held back** by rules, controls, or other limitations. It
typically describes freedom, power, activity, or imagination that is allowed to develop or operate without interference.

**Synonym:** **unrestricted** (not limited by rules or conditions); **unfettered** (free from restraints; formal);
**unconstrained** (not held back by external limits); **unchecked** (not controlled or restrained; often suggesting
harmful excess)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used before a noun or after a linking verb: **untrammelled freedom**, **untrammelled power**,
**Her imagination remained untrammelled**. Often followed by **by** to name a potential restriction: **untrammelled by
convention**. **Untrammelled** is the usual British spelling; **untrammeled** is the usual American spelling. It is not
normally used in comparative or superlative forms.

**Example:** *The children were encouraged to give their imaginations untrammelled freedom.* — “On encourageait les
enfants à laisser libre cours à leur imagination.”

Another example: *The new law gave the president almost untrammelled power.* — “La nouvelle loi a donné au président un
pouvoir presque sans limites.”

**German:** **uneingeschränkt / ungehindert / ungezügelt** · **French:** **sans entraves / sans limites / sans
contraintes**

Useful nuance: **Untrammelled** is formal and relatively uncommon in everyday conversation; **unrestricted** or **free
from restrictions** is usually more natural. Its tone depends on context: **untrammelled creativity** can sound
positive, while **untrammelled power** often suggests a dangerous lack of checks or accountability. Despite its
appearance, it does not mean **untrampled** or “not walked on.”

## raucous

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **Raucous** means **loud, harsh, and often unpleasant to hear**. It describes voices, laughter, cries,
or other sounds, as well as people or gatherings that are noisy and boisterous.

**Synonym:** **harsh** (rough and unpleasant in sound); **raucous** ≈ **rowdy** (noisy and disorderly, especially of
people or gatherings); **boisterous** (noisy, energetic, and lively, not necessarily unpleasant)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used before a noun or after a linking verb: **raucous laughter**, **a raucous crowd**, **The
crowd grew raucous**. Comparative and superlative: **more raucous**, **most raucous**. The adverb is **raucously**; the
noun is **raucousness**.

**Example:** *Raucous laughter from the next room interrupted our conversation.* — “Des éclats de rire bruyants venant
de la pièce voisine ont interrompu notre conversation.”

Another example: *The fans grew increasingly raucous as the match drew to a close.* — “Les supporters sont devenus de
plus en plus bruyants à mesure que le match approchait de sa fin.”

**German:** **rau und laut / lärmend / grölend** · **French:** **rauque / bruyant / tapageur**

Useful nuance: **Raucous** is stronger and more descriptive than **loud**: it suggests a rough, harsh sound or noisy,
uninhibited behaviour. **Raucous laughter** may convey cheerful high spirits rather than disapproval, while **a raucous
voice** emphasizes an unpleasantly rough sound. Common combinations include **raucous cheers**, **raucous laughter**,
**a raucous crowd**, and **a raucous party**.

## to field something

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **To field something** means to receive and deal with it, especially a **question**, **request**,
**inquiry**, or **complaint**. It often suggests responding competently or promptly, sometimes under pressure. In sport,
it also means to catch or stop a ball, or to select and put a team or player into a game. In politics, an organization
can **field a candidate** by putting them forward for election.

**Synonym:** **handle** (deal with something); **answer** (respond to a question or inquiry); **deal with** (take
responsibility for responding to something)

**Grammar:** Usually a transitive verb: **to field something**. Regular forms: **fields**, **fielded**, **fielding**.
Common combinations include **field questions**, **field calls**, **field inquiries**, and **field complaints**. The
source can be introduced with **from**: **field questions from reporters**. In cricket and baseball, it can also be
intransitive: **Our team is fielding first**.

**Example:** *The spokesperson fielded questions from reporters after the announcement.* — “Le porte-parole a répondu
aux questions des journalistes après l’annonce.”

Another example: *Our support team fields hundreds of calls every day.* — “Notre équipe d’assistance traite des
centaines d’appels chaque jour.”

**German:** **Fragen beantworten / Anfragen oder Beschwerden bearbeiten / eine Mannschaft aufstellen** · **French:**
**répondre à des questions / traiter des demandes ou des réclamations / aligner une équipe**

Useful nuance: **Field** emphasizes receiving and handling what comes your way, rather than merely giving an answer.
**Fielding questions** may involve unexpected or difficult questions, but does not necessarily imply difficulty or a
successful resolution. It is especially common in professional, media, and public-speaking contexts. In sport, **field a
ball** means to catch or stop it and usually return it; **field a team** means to put a team into a match or
competition. The noun **field** commonly means an area of land, a sports ground, or an area of study or work.

## diminutive

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **diminutive** means **very small**, especially compared with what is usual or expected. It often
describes a person’s height or an object’s size. In grammar, a **diminutive** is a word or word form that suggests
smallness, affection, or familiarity, such as **duckling** or **Tommy**.

**Synonym:** **tiny** (very small; more common in everyday English); **small** (neutral and general); **petite**
(usually describing a woman with a small, slender build); **miniature** (much smaller than the usual version)

**Grammar:** Adjective: **diminutive – more diminutive – most diminutive**, though comparisons are uncommon. It can
appear before a noun or after a linking verb: **a diminutive figure**; **She was diminutive in stature**. It is also a
countable noun in grammar: **a diminutive**, plural **diminutives**. Common patterns include **a diminutive of a name**
and **a diminutive suffix**, such as **-let** in **piglet**.

**Example:** *A diminutive woman stood behind the counter.* — “Une femme de très petite taille se tenait derrière le
comptoir.”

Another example: *Tommy is a diminutive of Thomas.* — “Tommy est un diminutif de Thomas.”

**German:** **winzig / sehr klein; das Diminutiv / die Verkleinerungsform** · **French:** **tout petit / de petite
taille ; un diminutif**

Useful nuance: **Diminutive** is more formal or literary than **small** or **tiny**. When describing a person, it
usually refers to physical size, not age or importance, though context can make it sound patronizing. In grammar, a
**diminutive** does not always indicate literal smallness: a name such as **Tommy** can express familiarity or affection
even when used for an adult. Depending on the language and context, diminutives can also express contempt or make
something seem less important.

## cavort

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **cavort** means **to jump, dance, or play energetically and happily**, often in a lively or
uncontrolled way. **Cavorting** is the **-ing** form. **Cavort with someone** can also mean to spend time having fun
with someone, especially in a way that suggests sexual involvement or attracts disapproval.

**Synonym:** **frolic** (play happily and energetically); **romp** (play or run around energetically); **prance** (move
with lively, springing steps)

**Grammar:** Intransitive verb: **cavort – cavorts – cavorted – cavorting**. It does not take a direct object. Common
patterns include **cavort around**, **cavort in the water**, and **cavort with someone**. **Cavorting** is used in
continuous tenses, as in **The children were cavorting**, and as a gerund, as in **Their cavorting woke us up**.

**Example:** *The children were cavorting in the waves.* — “Les enfants s’ébattaient dans les vagues.”

Another example: *The actor was photographed cavorting with a mystery woman.* — “L’acteur a été photographié en train de
batifoler avec une inconnue.”

**German:** **herumtollen / ausgelassen herumtanzen; mit jemandem tändeln** · **French:** **s’ébattre / gambader ;
batifoler avec quelqu’un**

Useful nuance: **Cavort** is a vivid, relatively uncommon word, often used humorously or in descriptive writing. For
children or animals, it usually suggests carefree physical play. In celebrity reporting, **cavorting with someone**
often insinuates flirtation or sexual activity without stating exactly what happened. It does not necessarily mean
having sex. For neutral everyday descriptions, **play**, **run around**, or **dance** may be more natural.

## a globule

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** A **globule** is a very small, rounded drop or ball of a substance, especially a liquid or something
soft. The word is often used to describe fat, oil, or other substances seen as separate tiny spheres, particularly in
scientific or descriptive writing.

**Synonym:** **droplet** (a very small drop of liquid); **bead** (a small, rounded drop, especially on a surface);
**tiny sphere** (a small ball-shaped object)

**Grammar:** Countable noun: **a globule – globules**. Common patterns include **a globule of oil**, **globules of
fat**, and **fat globules**. Use **of** to name the substance: **a globule of mercury**. The related adjective
**globular** means “roughly spherical” or “shaped like a small ball.”

**Example:** *Tiny globules of fat floated on the surface of the soup.* — “De minuscules globules de graisse flottaient
à la surface de la soupe.”

Another example: *Under the microscope, the oil appeared as separate globules.* — “Au microscope, l’huile apparaissait
sous forme de globules distincts.”

**German:** **das Kügelchen / das Tröpfchen** · **French:** **le globule / la petite goutte**

Useful nuance: **Globule** emphasizes a small, rounded shape, often with a distinct boundary. **Droplet** is more common
in everyday English and refers specifically to liquid; **globule** can also describe a tiny rounded mass of a soft or
solid substance. French **globule** also occurs in **globule rouge** and **globule blanc**, but the usual English terms
are **red blood cell** and **white blood cell**, not “red globule” and “white globule.”

## a mane

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** A **mane** is the long, thick hair that grows along the top of a horse’s neck or around a male lion’s
head and neck. Informally, it can also mean a person’s long, thick hair, especially when it looks full or wild.

**Synonym:** **long neck hair** (a plain description for a horse); **thick hair** (for a person); **a mass of hair** (a
descriptive alternative in the figurative sense)

**Grammar:** Countable noun: **a mane – manes**. Common combinations include **a horse’s mane**, **a lion’s mane**, **a
flowing mane**, and **a shaggy mane**. For a person’s hair, use **a mane of hair**, often with an adjective: **a mane of
curly hair**. Pronunciation: **/meɪn/**, exactly like **main**.

**Example:** *She brushed the horse’s long mane.* — “Elle a brossé la longue crinière du cheval.”

Another example: *He had a wild mane of curly red hair.* — “Il avait une crinière ébouriffée de cheveux roux et
bouclés.”

**German:** **die Mähne** · **French:** **la crinière**

Useful nuance: A horse’s **mane** runs along its neck; the long hair at the other end of its body is its **tail**. A
male lion’s **mane** surrounds its head and neck. When used for a person, **mane** is vivid and descriptive rather than
neutral: it suggests abundant, noticeable hair, not simply any hairstyle.

## tawdry

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **tawdry** means **showy but cheap and of poor quality**, especially when describing clothes, jewellery,
or decorations. It can also mean **sordid**, **shameful**, or **morally unpleasant**, as in a scandal or an affair.

**Synonym:** **tacky** (cheap-looking or in poor taste; informal); **gaudy** (too bright or showy, but not necessarily
cheap); **cheap-looking** (appearing inexpensive and of poor quality); **sordid** (morally unpleasant or shameful)

**Grammar:** Adjective: **tawdry – tawdrier – tawdriest**. It can come before a noun or after a linking verb: **tawdry
jewellery**, **tawdry decorations**, **a tawdry scandal**; **The room looked tawdry**. Related forms include the adverb
**tawdrily** and the noun **tawdriness**.

**Example:** *The shop window was filled with tawdry jewellery.* — “La vitrine était remplie de bijoux de pacotille.”

Another example: *The newspaper exposed the tawdry details of the scandal.* — “Le journal a révélé les détails sordides
du scandale.”

**German:** **billig und geschmacklos / kitschig; schäbig / anrüchig** · **French:** **de pacotille / clinquant ;
sordide / peu reluisant**

Useful nuance: **Tawdry** is strongly disapproving and somewhat literary or journalistic. For objects, it suggests an
attempt to look attractive or impressive that instead looks cheap and tasteless—not simply something inexpensive.
**Gaudy** emphasizes excessive colour or decoration, while **tawdry** adds a sense of poor quality. In **a tawdry
affair** or **a tawdry scandal**, the criticism concerns shameful or sordid behaviour rather than physical appearance.

## blasé

**CEFR:** roughly **C2**.

**Definition:** **blasé** means **not easily impressed, excited, or concerned**, especially because you have experienced
something many times before. It often suggests that someone treats something interesting or important as ordinary. It
can also describe an overly casual attitude toward risks or responsibilities.

**Synonym:** **unimpressed** (not impressed by something); **nonchalant** (casual and apparently unconcerned); **jaded**
(no longer enthusiastic because of too much experience); **indifferent** (not interested or concerned)

**Grammar:** Adjective: **blasé – more blasé – most blasé**. Used before a noun or after a linking verb: **a blasé
attitude**, **She seemed blasé**. A common pattern is **be blasé about something / doing something**. The spelling
**blase**, without the accent, is also used in English. Pronounced roughly **blah-ZAY** /blɑːˈzeɪ/.

**Example:** *After years of travelling for work, she had become blasé about staying in luxury hotels.* — “Après des
années de déplacements professionnels, elle était devenue blasée à l’idée de séjourner dans des hôtels de luxe.”

Another example: *You shouldn't be so blasé about sharing your personal information online.* — “Tu ne devrais pas
prendre autant à la légère le fait de partager tes informations personnelles en ligne.”

**German:** **blasiert / abgeklärt / gleichgültig; sorglos** · **French:** **blasé ; désinvolte / insouciant**

Useful nuance: **Blasé** usually implies a lack of enthusiasm or concern where others might expect excitement or
caution. Unlike **bored**, it often suggests familiarity or extensive experience rather than temporary lack of interest.
Unlike **calm**, it can sound critical: **blasé about safety** suggests not taking safety seriously enough. German
**blasiert** can imply arrogance more strongly than English **blasé** does.

## groggy

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **groggy** means **not fully awake or alert, and feeling weak or unsteady**, especially just after
waking up or because of illness, medication, alcohol, or a blow to the head.

**Synonym:** **drowsy** (sleepy); **dazed** (unable to think clearly, often after a shock or injury); ** woozy**
(informal: dizzy, weak, or slightly confused)

**Grammar:** Adjective: **groggy – groggier – groggiest**. Commonly used after **feel**, **be**, or **wake up**: **feel
groggy**, **still be groggy**, **wake up groggy**. To name the cause, use **groggy from something**: **groggy from lack
of sleep**, **groggy from the medication**. Related forms are the adverb **groggily** and the noun **grogginess**.

**Example:** *I felt groggy for an hour after waking up.* — “Je me suis senti dans le brouillard pendant une heure après
mon réveil.”

Another example: *The medication made her feel groggy.* — “Le médicament l’a rendue somnolente.”

**German:** **benommen / noch ganz verschlafen** · **French:** **dans le brouillard / vaseux / somnolent**

Useful nuance: **Groggy** is stronger than simply **tired**: it suggests that you cannot think clearly or move as
steadily as usual. Someone who is **sleepy** wants to sleep; someone who is **groggy** feels only partly alert and may
be unsteady. It is common in everyday descriptions of waking up or recovering from an anaesthetic. French also uses
**groggy**, especially for someone dazed by a blow or shock.

## blandly

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **blandly** means **calmly and without showing much emotion or interest**, especially when speaking. It
often describes someone saying something surprising, rude, or dishonest as though it were perfectly ordinary. It can
also mean **in a dull or uninteresting way**, without any distinctive character.

**Synonym:** **unemotionally** (without showing emotion); **impassively** (without revealing feelings or reactions);
**nonchalantly** (in a casually unconcerned way); **dully** (without interest or liveliness)

**Grammar:** Adverb formed from the adjective **bland** + **-ly**. Comparative and superlative: **more blandly – most
blandly**. It commonly modifies verbs of speaking: **say blandly**, **reply blandly**, **remark blandly**, **assure
someone blandly**. It can come before the verb or after it: **She blandly denied it**; **She denied it blandly**.

**Example:** *“I have no idea what you mean,” he replied blandly.* — « Je ne vois pas du tout ce que vous voulez dire »,
a-t-il répondu d’un ton neutre.

Another example: *The hotel rooms were blandly decorated in beige and grey.* — « Les chambres de l’hôtel étaient
décorées sans caractère, dans des tons beiges et gris. »

**German:** **ausdruckslos / gelassen; nichtssagend / langweilig** · **French:** **d’un ton neutre / impassiblement ;
sans caractère / de façon fade**

Useful nuance: **Blandly** often creates a contrast between someone’s calm manner and what they are saying or doing.
**He blandly denied everything** may suggest an annoyingly unconcerned or deliberately innocent manner, not merely a
lack of emotion. Unlike **politely**, it does not necessarily imply good manners or consideration. Although **bland**
commonly describes food with little flavour, **blandly** is much more often used for speech, behaviour, or an
uninteresting style.

## an inquest

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** An **inquest** is an official investigation, usually led by a coroner, into the cause and circumstances
of a death, especially one that was sudden, unexplained, or violent. Informally, it can also mean a detailed examination
of something that went wrong, such as a defeat or failure.

**Synonym:** **investigation** (a general inquiry into facts or causes); **inquiry** (an official examination of an
event); **post-mortem** (an informal review of a failure; also a medical examination of a body)

**Grammar:** Countable noun: **an inquest – inquests**. Common patterns include **an inquest into a death**, **hold an
inquest**, **open an inquest**, and **at the inquest**. For the informal sense, use **an inquest into a failure** or
**an inquest into what went wrong**.

**Example:** *The coroner opened an inquest into the death of the young woman.* — “Le coroner a ouvert une enquête sur
les causes du décès de la jeune femme.”

Another example: *After the team's crushing defeat, the inevitable inquest began.* — “Après la lourde défaite de
l’équipe, l’inévitable analyse des causes de l’échec a commencé.”

**German:** **gerichtliche Untersuchung eines Todesfalls / Untersuchung der Ursachen eines Misserfolgs** · **French:**
**enquête judiciaire sur les causes d’un décès / analyse des causes d’un échec**

Useful nuance: An **inquest** establishes facts about a death; it is not a criminal trial and does not decide whether
someone is guilty of a crime. The term is especially associated with legal systems that have coroners, including England
and Wales. An **autopsy** or **post-mortem examination** is a medical examination of a body and may provide evidence for
an inquest, but it is not the inquest itself. The informal sense often suggests a searching, sometimes blame-focused
review of what went wrong.

## dazedly

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **dazedly** means **in a confused or stunned way**, without being fully aware of what is happening or
able to think clearly. Someone may act dazedly after a shock, a blow to the head, or waking from deep sleep.

**Synonym:** **groggily** (in a weak or mentally unclear way, especially from sleep, illness, or medication); **in a
daze** (feeling confused or stunned); **confusedly** (without understanding clearly)

**Grammar:** Adverb formed from the adjective **dazed** + **-ly**. It describes how someone acts: **look around
dazedly**, **stare dazedly**, **blink dazedly**. It usually follows the verb or its object: **She shook her head
dazedly**. The related verb is **to daze someone** (“to stun or confuse someone”).

**Example:** *He sat up and looked around dazedly.* — “Il s’est redressé et a regardé autour de lui d’un air hébété.”

Another example: *After hearing the news, she stared dazedly at the screen.* — “Après avoir appris la nouvelle, elle a
fixé l’écran d’un air abasourdi.”

**German:** **benommen / wie benommen** · **French:** **d’un air hébété / d’un air abasourdi**

Useful nuance: **Dazedly** suggests temporary mental fog or stunned confusion, not simply a lack of knowledge or
understanding. It does not necessarily mean feeling physically dizzy: **dizzily** emphasizes a sensation of spinning or
losing balance. **Dazedly** is more typical of descriptive writing than everyday conversation, where **in a daze** or
**feeling dazed** is often more natural.
