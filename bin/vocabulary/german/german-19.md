## etwas (Akkusativ) rechtfertigen / sich (Akkusativ) rechtfertigen

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **Rechtfertigen** means **to justify** something: to give reasons showing that an action, decision, or
claim is reasonable, acceptable, or necessary. It can also mean that something provides sufficient grounds for an action
or judgment, as in **Die Ergebnisse rechtfertigen den Aufwand** (“The results justify the effort”). Reflexive **sich
rechtfertigen** means **to justify oneself** or **to defend one’s actions**, especially in response to criticism or
accusations.

**Synonym:** **begründen** (to give reasons for something; more neutral and not necessarily defensive); **verteidigen**
(to defend an action, position, or person against criticism); **legitimieren** (to legitimize; more formal, often
involving authority or accepted standards)

**Grammar:** Regular verb: **rechtfertigt – rechtfertigte – hat gerechtfertigt**. It is not separable; the past
participle is **gerechtfertigt**, and the infinitive with **zu** is **zu rechtfertigen**. Common patterns include
**etwas (Akkusativ) rechtfertigen** (“to justify something”), **etwas mit etwas (Dativ) rechtfertigen** (“to justify
something by citing something”), **sich (Akkusativ) für etwas (Akkusativ) rechtfertigen** (“to justify oneself for
something”), and **sich vor jemandem (Dativ) rechtfertigen** (“to justify oneself to someone”). The reflexive pronoun
changes with the subject: **ich rechtfertige mich**, **du rechtfertigst dich**, **sie rechtfertigt sich**.

**Example:** *Die Firma rechtfertigte die Preiserhöhung mit gestiegenen Energiekosten.* — “The company justified the
price increase by citing higher energy costs.”

Another example: *Du musst dich nicht für jede Entscheidung rechtfertigen.* — “You don’t have to justify yourself for
every decision.”

**English:** **to justify / to justify oneself** · **French:** **justifier / se justifier**

Useful nuance: **Rechtfertigen** goes beyond simply explaining why something happened: it presents something as
justified or defensible. **Begründen** can neutrally explain the reasons for a decision, while **sich rechtfertigen**
often suggests that someone feels challenged or accused. Justifying an action does not necessarily mean apologizing for
it; **sich entschuldigen** means “to apologize.” The phrase **Das rechtfertigt nicht …** (“That does not justify …”)
rejects a reason as insufficient. **Gerechtfertigt** is also commonly used as an adjective meaning “justified” or
“warranted,” as in **gerechtfertigte Kritik** (“justified criticism”). The related noun is **die Rechtfertigung**
(“justification”).

## das Gebiet

**CEFR:** roughly **B1**.

**Definition:** **Gebiet** means an **area**, **region**, or **territory**: a part of a country, landscape, or other
geographical space. It also means a **field** or **area of activity or knowledge**, such as medicine, research, or
technology.

**Synonym:** **Region** (region; a geographical area, often with shared characteristics); **Gegend** (area or locality;
often used when describing surroundings); **Territorium** (territory; especially an area under political control);
**Bereich** (area or field; especially a sphere of activity or knowledge)

**Grammar:** Neuter noun: **das Gebiet**, genitive **des Gebiets / des Gebietes**, plural **die Gebiete**. Common
patterns include **in einem Gebiet (Dativ)** (“in an area”), **in ein Gebiet (Akkusativ)** (“into an area”), and **auf
dem Gebiet der Medizin / Technik** (“in the field of medicine / technology”). For expertise, use **auf einem Gebiet
(Dativ)**, as in **auf diesem Gebiet erfahren sein** (“to be experienced in this field”).

**Example:** *Nach dem starken Regen wurden mehrere Gebiete überschwemmt.* — “Several areas were flooded after the heavy
rain.”

Another example: *Sie ist Expertin auf dem Gebiet der erneuerbaren Energien.* — “She is an expert in the field of
renewable energy.”

**English:** **area / region / territory / field** · **French:** **zone / région / territoire / domaine**

Useful nuance: **Gebiet** often presents an area as a distinct geographical unit, without necessarily implying official
borders. **Gegend** is more typical for someone's surroundings or a loosely defined locality, while **Territorium**
emphasizes political control or possession. In the figurative sense, **auf diesem Gebiet** means “in this field,”
whereas **in diesem Gebiet** usually refers to a geographical location. Common compounds include **das Wohngebiet**
(“residential area”), **das Naturschutzgebiet** (“nature reserve”), and **das Fachgebiet** (“specialist field”).

## die Eitelkeit

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **Eitelkeit** means **vanity**: excessive pride in one’s appearance, abilities, or importance, often
combined with a desire to be admired. It can concern physical attractiveness, but also intelligence, professional
success, or social status. In older, literary, or religious language, it can also mean **futility** or **the transience
of worldly things**.

**Synonym:** **Selbstgefälligkeit** (self-satisfaction or smugness; being overly pleased with oneself);
**Geltungsdrang** (a strong desire for recognition or attention); **Gefallsucht** (an excessive desire to please or be
admired; strongly disapproving)

**Grammar:** Feminine noun: **die Eitelkeit**, genitive **der Eitelkeit**, plural **die Eitelkeiten**. Usually
uncountable when referring to the character trait; the plural can refer to individual displays of vanity or personal
sensitivities connected with pride. Common patterns include **aus Eitelkeit** (“out of vanity”), **jemandes Eitelkeit
verletzen** (“to wound someone’s vanity”), and **jemandes Eitelkeit schmeicheln (Dativ)** (“to flatter someone’s
vanity”), as in **Das Lob schmeichelt seiner Eitelkeit**. The related adjective is **eitel** (“vain”).

**Example:** *Aus Eitelkeit wollte er nicht zugeben, dass er einen Fehler gemacht hatte.* — “Out of vanity, he did not
want to admit that he had made a mistake.”

Another example: *Das Kompliment schmeichelte ihrer Eitelkeit.* — “The compliment flattered her vanity.”

**English:** **vanity** · **French:** **vanité**

Useful nuance: **Eitelkeit** usually has a critical or disapproving tone, though it can also be used lightly or
humorously. It is not limited to concern about appearance: **intellektuelle Eitelkeit** means “intellectual vanity.”
Unlike **Stolz** (“pride”), which can be justified and positive, **Eitelkeit** suggests excessive self-regard or a need
for admiration. **Persönliche Eitelkeiten** often refers to wounded pride or self-important concerns that interfere with
cooperation. The older sense of “futility” or “transience” occurs especially in literary and religious contexts, as in
**die Eitelkeit der Welt** (“the vanity of the world”).

## unverzeihlich

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **unverzeihlich** means **unforgivable**: so wrong, hurtful, or serious that it cannot or should not be
forgiven. It can also mean **inexcusable**, especially when describing a mistake, omission, or failure that should
clearly have been avoided.

**Synonym:** **unverzeihbar** (unforgivable; very close in meaning); **unentschuldbar** (inexcusable; emphasizing that
no excuse can justify something); **nicht zu verzeihen** (not to be forgiven; a common equivalent phrase)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used attributively, predicatively, and adverbially: **ein unverzeihlicher Fehler** (“an
unforgivable mistake”), **Das ist unverzeihlich** (“That is unforgivable”), **etwas unverzeihlich vernachlässigen** (“to
neglect something inexcusably”). Attributive forms take normal adjective endings. Comparative **unverzeihlicher**,
superlative **am unverzeihlichsten**; these forms are possible when comparing degrees of seriousness, though the basic
meaning is often treated as absolute. The related verb is **jemandem (Dativ) etwas (Akkusativ) verzeihen** (“to forgive
someone for something”).

**Example:** *Dass er ihr Vertrauen missbraucht hat, ist für sie unverzeihlich.* — “For her, his abuse of her trust is
unforgivable.”

Another example: *Bei einer so wichtigen Prüfung war dieser Fehler unverzeihlich.* — “In such an important exam, this
mistake was inexcusable.”

**English:** **unforgivable / inexcusable** · **French:** **impardonnable / inexcusable**

Useful nuance: **unverzeihlich** expresses strong disapproval, but does not always mean that forgiveness is literally
impossible. **Ein unverzeihlicher Fehler** may simply be a very serious or avoidable mistake. **Unentschuldbar** focuses
more on the absence of a valid excuse, while **unverzeihlich** foregrounds forgiveness or moral judgment. Common
combinations include **ein unverzeihlicher Verrat** (“an unforgivable betrayal”), **eine unverzeihliche Nachlässigkeit**
(“inexcusable negligence”), and **ein unverzeihlicher Fehler** (“an unforgivable mistake”).

## anschließend

**CEFR:** roughly **B1**.

**Definition:** **anschließend** means **afterwards** or **then**: after the event or action just mentioned, as the next
step in a sequence. As an adjective, it can also mean **subsequent** or **following**, as in **die anschließende
Diskussion** (“the subsequent discussion”).

**Synonym:** **danach** (afterwards; a very common, neutral equivalent); **daraufhin** (then or thereupon; often
suggesting a reaction or consequence); **im Anschluss** (afterwards; a common equivalent phrase)

**Grammar:** Usually a temporal adverb, with no endings: **Wir essen und gehen anschließend spazieren** (“We eat and
then go for a walk”). When it begins a main clause, the finite verb comes immediately after it: **Anschließend gehen wir
spazieren**. It is also used as an attributive adjective with normal endings: **ein anschließendes Gespräch**, **die
anschließende Besprechung**. It has no usual comparative or superlative in this temporal sense. The related phrase **im
Anschluss an etwas (Akkusativ)** means “following something” or “after something”: **im Anschluss an die Sitzung**
(“after the meeting”).

**Example:** *Wir besichtigen zuerst das Museum und gehen anschließend etwas essen.* — “First we visit the museum, and
afterwards we go for something to eat.”

Another example: *Der Vortrag und die anschließende Diskussion dauerten insgesamt zwei Stunden.* — “The lecture and the
subsequent discussion lasted two hours in total.”

**English:** **afterwards / then / subsequent** · **French:** **ensuite / après / suivant**

Useful nuance: **anschließend** links an action closely to what came before and often presents it as the next item in a
planned sequence. It does not necessarily mean “immediately afterwards”; use **unmittelbar anschließend** or **direkt
anschließend** to make that explicit. Compared with **danach**, it is somewhat more typical of organized descriptions,
instructions, and written accounts, though it is also common in speech. Unlike **schließlich** (“finally”), it does not
imply that this is the last step. In descriptions of space, adjectival **anschließend** can also mean “adjoining,” as in
**die daran anschließenden Räume** (“the adjoining rooms”).

## unterschwellig

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **unterschwellig** means **underlying**, **subtle**, or **implicit**: present or perceptible without
being openly expressed or clearly noticeable, especially of feelings, tensions, criticism, or messages. In psychology
and descriptions of perception, it also means **subliminal**: below the threshold of conscious awareness.

**Synonym:** **unterschwellenhaft** is not a usual equivalent; **latent** (latent or underlying; present but not openly
apparent); **unausgesprochen** (unspoken; not explicitly expressed); **subliminal** (subliminal; especially in
psychology and perception)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used attributively, predicatively, and adverbially: **eine unterschwellige Spannung** (“an
underlying tension”), **Die Kritik bleibt unterschwellig** (“The criticism remains implicit”), **jemanden unterschwellig
beeinflussen** (“to influence someone subtly or subliminally”). Attributive forms take normal adjective endings.
Comparative **unterschwelliger**, superlative **am unterschwelligsten**; these are possible in the everyday sense of
“subtle,” but uncommon in the technical sense of “below the threshold of perception.” The word is related to **die
Schwelle** (“threshold”).

**Example:** *Zwischen den beiden war eine unterschwellige Spannung zu spüren.* — “There was an underlying tension
between the two of them.”

Another example: *In seinen Bemerkungen schwang immer eine unterschwellige Kritik mit.* — “His remarks always carried an
undertone of criticism.”

**English:** **underlying / subtle / implicit / subliminal** · **French:** **latent / sous-jacent / implicite /
subliminal**

Useful nuance: In everyday use, **unterschwellig** often describes something that you can sense without its being stated
directly. It does not necessarily mean that something is deliberately concealed or completely unconscious.
**Unterschwellige Kritik** is indirect criticism, while **unterschwellige Angst** is an underlying fear. In the stricter
psychological sense, **unterschwellige Reize** (“subliminal stimuli”) fall below the threshold of conscious perception.
Common combinations include **unterschwellige Aggression** (“underlying aggression”), **unterschwellige Botschaften**
(“implicit or subliminal messages”), and **unterschwellige Spannungen** (“underlying tensions”).

## schwachsinnig

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **schwachsinnig** means **stupid**, **idiotic**, or **absurd**: showing very poor judgment or making no
sense, especially of ideas, remarks, plans, or actions. It is strongly disapproving. Historically, it was also used to
mean **intellectually disabled**; that use is now outdated and offensive.

**Synonym:** **unsinnig** (nonsensical; a less insulting alternative focused on lack of sense); **blödsinnig** (idiotic
or absurd; similarly dismissive); **hirnrissig** (harebrained or ridiculous; colloquial and strongly critical)

**Grammar:** Adjective, used attributively, predicatively, and adverbially: **eine schwachsinnige Idee** (“an idiotic
idea”), **Das ist schwachsinnig** (“That is absurd”), **sich schwachsinnig verhalten** (“to behave idiotically”).
Attributive forms take normal adjective endings. Comparative **schwachsinniger**, superlative **am schwachsinnigsten**.
The related noun is **der Schwachsinn**, commonly meaning “nonsense” or “idiocy.”

**Example:** *Es wäre schwachsinnig, für diesen kurzen Weg das Auto zu nehmen.* — “It would be ridiculous to take the
car for such a short journey.”

Another example: *Sie hielt den Vorschlag für völlig schwachsinnig.* — “She thought the proposal was completely
idiotic.”

**English:** **stupid / idiotic / absurd** · **French:** **stupide / idiot / absurde**

Useful nuance: **schwachsinnig** is common in informal speech but sounds harsh and dismissive. Calling a proposal
**schwachsinnig** is much stronger than calling it **unvernünftig** (“unreasonable”) or **wenig sinnvoll** (“not very
sensible”). Applied directly to a person, it is an insult and can echo the word’s offensive historical use for
intellectual disability. Avoid it as a neutral description of someone’s abilities; **eine Person mit einer
intellektuellen Behinderung** is an appropriate modern expression when that meaning is intended. Common combinations
include **eine schwachsinnige Idee**, **eine schwachsinnige Frage**, and **völlig schwachsinnig**.

## nach Strich und Faden

**CEFR:** roughly **C1**.

**Definition:** **nach Strich und Faden** means **thoroughly**, **completely**, or **in every possible way**. This
emphatic idiom describes an action carried out to an extreme degree, especially cheating, lying, or criticizing. It can
also intensify positive actions such as pampering someone. The usual expression includes **nach**, rather than simply
**Strich und Faden**.

**Synonym:** **gründlich** (thoroughly; a neutral equivalent in many contexts); **durch und durch** (completely or
through and through; suitable in some contexts); **nach allen Regeln der Kunst** (with every trick in the book or in a
thoroughly accomplished way; emphasizes how something is done)

**Grammar:** A fixed adverbial phrase introduced by **nach**, which takes the dative. Both nouns appear without articles
in this idiom, and the wording does not change. It commonly modifies verbs with an accusative object: **jemanden
(Akkusativ) nach Strich und Faden belügen / betrügen / verwöhnen** (“to lie to / cheat / pamper someone thoroughly”). It
also occurs with **jemanden nach Strich und Faden ausnehmen** (“to fleece someone completely”). The component nouns are
**der Strich**, plural **die Striche**, and **der Faden**, plural **die Fäden**.

**Example:** *Der Händler hat uns nach Strich und Faden betrogen.* — “The dealer cheated us every which way.”

Another example: *Bei ihren Großeltern wird sie nach Strich und Faden verwöhnt.* — “Her grandparents pamper her to no
end.”

**English:** **thoroughly / completely / every which way** · **French:** **de fond en comble / complètement / sur toute
la ligne**

Useful nuance: **nach Strich und Faden** is vivid and emphatic, especially common in conversational language and
expressive writing. With **belügen** or **betrügen**, it suggests extensive, shameless deception, not merely one small
lie or minor trick. With **verwöhnen**, it means pampering someone lavishly. It is not a general replacement for
**gründlich** in neutral instructions: **etwas gründlich reinigen** is the ordinary way to say “clean something
thoroughly.” Separately, **der Strich** commonly means “line” or “stroke,” and **der Faden** means “thread”; the idiom
should not be translated literally.

## jemanden/etwas (Akkusativ) in etwas (Akkusativ) einwickeln

**CEFR:** roughly **B1**.

**Definition:** **einwickeln** means **to wrap** or **to wrap up**: to cover or surround something or someone with
paper, fabric, or another flexible material. Figuratively, **jemanden einwickeln** means **to charm someone into doing
something** or **to win someone over**, often through flattery or clever persuasion.

**Synonym:** **einschlagen** (to wrap; especially in paper or cloth); **einpacken** (to wrap or pack; a common
equivalent for gifts and other objects); **um den Finger wickeln** (to twist someone around one’s little finger; a
figurative equivalent involving charm or manipulation)

**Grammar:** Separable, regular verb: **wickelt ein**, **wickelte ein**, **hat eingewickelt**. In a main clause, **ein**
goes to the end: **Sie wickelt das Geschenk ein** (“She wraps the gift”). The infinitive with **zu** is
**einzuwickeln**. The person or thing being wrapped takes the accusative; the wrapping material is commonly introduced
by **in + Akkusativ**: **ein Geschenk in Papier einwickeln**, **ein Baby in eine Decke einwickeln**. Reflexive use is
also possible: **sich in eine Decke einwickeln** (“to wrap oneself in a blanket”). The figurative sense also takes an
accusative object: **jemanden einwickeln**.

**Example:** *Ich wickle das Geschenk in buntes Papier ein.* — “I wrap the gift in colorful paper.”

Another example: *Lass dich nicht von seinen netten Worten einwickeln.* — “Don’t let him win you over with his nice
words.”

**English:** **to wrap / to wrap up / to charm someone into something** · **French:** **envelopper / emballer /
embobiner quelqu’un**

Useful nuance: **einwickeln** emphasizes putting a covering around something, whereas **einpacken** can also mean
putting things into a bag, suitcase, or box. **Ein Geschenk einwickeln** usually means wrapping it in paper; **ein Baby
einwickeln** usually means wrapping it in a blanket or cloth. Figurative **jemanden einwickeln** is colloquial and often
suggests that someone uses charm or flattery to get their way. It can sound playful or critical, depending on the
context.

## ausgesprochen

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **ausgesprochen** most commonly means **very**, **extremely**, or **particularly** when used to
intensify an adjective or adverb: **ausgesprochen freundlich** (“very friendly”). As an adjective, it can mean
**definite**, **distinct**, or **out-and-out**, describing a clear characteristic or strong preference. It is also the
past participle of **aussprechen**, meaning **pronounced**, **spoken**, or **expressed**.

**Synonym:** **sehr** (very; the most neutral equivalent for the intensifier); **besonders** (particularly; often
suitable, though it can imply comparison); **äußerst** (extremely; a strong, somewhat more formal intensifier);
**regelrecht** (downright or outright; suitable for some adjectival uses)

**Grammar:** As an intensifying adverb, **ausgesprochen** is invariable and normally stands directly before the
adjective or adverb it modifies: **ausgesprochen nett**, **ausgesprochen gut**. As an attributive adjective, it takes
the usual adjective endings: **ein ausgesprochener Tierfreund**, **eine ausgesprochene Vorliebe für etwas (Akkusativ)**.
As a participle, it belongs to the separable, irregular verb **aussprechen**: **spricht aus**, **sprach aus**, **hat
ausgesprochen**. Common verb patterns include **ein Wort (Akkusativ) aussprechen** (“to pronounce a word”) and **einen
Wunsch (Akkusativ) aussprechen** (“to express a wish”).

**Example:** *Die Mitarbeiterin war ausgesprochen hilfsbereit.* — “The employee was extremely helpful.”

Another example: *Er ist ein ausgesprochener Tierfreund.* — “He is a real animal lover.”

**English:** **very / extremely / definite / pronounced** · **French:** **très / extrêmement / véritable / prononcé**

Useful nuance: As an intensifier, **ausgesprochen** emphasizes that a quality is especially noticeable; it can sound a
little more deliberate or emphatic than **sehr**, but is common in both speech and writing. It works with positive and
negative qualities: **ausgesprochen angenehm** (“very pleasant”), **ausgesprochen schwierig** (“very difficult”). In
**ein ausgesprochener Tierfreund**, it means a genuine or particularly enthusiastic animal lover, not someone who has
been “pronounced” an animal lover. The literal participle meaning is clear in **Das Wort wird anders ausgesprochen**
(“The word is pronounced differently”). Do not confuse it with **ausgerechnet**, meaning “of all people/things” or
“precisely.”

## verwöhnt

**CEFR:** roughly **B1**.

**Definition:** **verwöhnt** means **spoiled**: accustomed to getting what one wants or receiving so much attention and
indulgence that one becomes demanding or unappreciative. It can also mean **pampered**, without necessarily implying bad
behavior. In contexts involving comfort, food, or service, it can describe someone who is **used to high standards**. It
is also the past participle of **verwöhnen** (“to spoil” or “to pamper”).

**Synonym:** **verzogen** (spoiled; especially describing a child’s bad behavior resulting from excessive indulgence);
**verhätschelt** (pampered or coddled; usually critical); **anspruchsvoll** (demanding or discerning; a partial
equivalent when someone is used to high standards)

**Grammar:** As an adjective, **verwöhnt** takes the usual endings before a noun: **ein verwöhntes Kind**, **verwöhnte
Gäste**. In predicative use, it has no ending: **Das Kind ist verwöhnt**. Comparative and superlative: **verwöhnter**,
**am verwöhntesten**. It is also the past participle of the inseparable, regular verb **verwöhnen**: **verwöhnt**,
**verwöhnte**, **hat verwöhnt**. The verb takes an accusative object: **jemanden (Akkusativ) verwöhnen**. What someone
is pampered with can be introduced by **mit + Dativ**: **jemanden mit gutem Essen verwöhnen**. **Von + Dativ** can
identify the source of someone’s high expectations: **Ich bin von zu Hause verwöhnt** (“I’m used to being spoiled at
home”).

**Example:** *Das verwöhnte Kind will immer seinen Willen bekommen.* — “The spoiled child always wants to get its own
way.”

Another example: *Im Urlaub haben wir uns richtig verwöhnt gefühlt.* — “We felt truly pampered on vacation.”

**English:** **spoiled / pampered / used to high standards** · **French:** **gâté / choyé / habitué à un certain
confort**

Useful nuance: **Verwöhnt** is often critical when it describes a person’s character, particularly in **ein verwöhntes
Kind** (“a spoiled child”). However, being **verwöhnt** can also be pleasant and positive: a hotel might promise to
pamper its guests. **Ich bin verwöhnt** can be a lighthearted admission that one is accustomed to excellent food,
comfort, or service, rather than a confession of bad behavior. **Verzogen** is more clearly negative and emphasizes poor
upbringing or manners, whereas **verwöhnt** can simply emphasize indulgence.

## etwas (Akkusativ) unterzeichnen

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **unterzeichnen** means **to sign** a document by adding one’s signature, usually to confirm agreement,
approval, or responsibility. It is especially common with contracts, agreements, declarations, and other official
documents. When used with a petition or public appeal, it means to express support by signing it.

**Synonym:** **unterschreiben** (to sign; the most common, neutral equivalent); **signieren** (to sign; especially
books, artworks, or other objects, rather than contracts)

**Grammar:** **Unterzeichnen** is an inseparable, regular verb: **unterzeichnet**, **unterzeichnete**, **hat
unterzeichnet**. It takes an accusative object: **etwas (Akkusativ) unterzeichnen**, for example **einen Vertrag
unterzeichnen** (“to sign a contract”), **ein Abkommen unterzeichnen** (“to sign an agreement”), or **eine Petition
unterzeichnen** (“to sign a petition”). The prefix **unter-** is unstressed and is not separated: **Sie unterzeichnet
den Vertrag**. The past participle has no **ge-**: **unterzeichnet**. The infinitive with **zu** is **zu
unterzeichnen**: **Der Vertrag ist noch zu unterzeichnen** (“The contract still needs to be signed”).

**Example:** *Beide Parteien haben den Vertrag unterzeichnet.* — “Both parties have signed the contract.”

Another example: *Mehr als tausend Menschen haben die Petition unterzeichnet.* — “More than a thousand people have
signed the petition.”

**English:** **to sign** · **French:** **signer**

Useful nuance: **Unterzeichnen** is more formal than **unterschreiben** and is particularly common in administrative,
legal, diplomatic, and news contexts. In everyday conversation, **Kannst du hier unterschreiben?** (“Can you sign
here?”) is more natural than using **unterzeichnen**. Both verbs can describe the same action; **unterzeichnen** does
not necessarily imply a special signing procedure. For signing a book or artwork, **signieren** is usually more
appropriate. The related noun **der Unterzeichner / die Unterzeichnerin** means **signatory** or **the person who
signs**.

## überwiegend

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** **überwiegend** means **mostly**, **predominantly**, or **for the most part**: it indicates that
something accounts for the larger part of a whole or is the main characteristic of something. As an adjective before a
noun, it means **predominant** or **greater**, especially in expressions such as **die überwiegende Mehrheit** (“the
vast majority”).

**Synonym:** **größtenteils** (mostly or for the most part; a close equivalent); **hauptsächlich** (mainly; emphasizes
the main component, activity, or focus); **vorwiegend** (predominantly; very close in meaning); **meist** (mostly or
usually; suitable in many contexts, but can refer more specifically to frequency)

**Grammar:** As an adverb, **überwiegend** is invariable: **überwiegend positiv**, **überwiegend zu Hause arbeiten**. As
an attributive adjective, it takes the usual adjective endings: **die überwiegende Mehrheit**, **der überwiegende
Teil**. It is derived from the present participle of the inseparable, irregular verb **überwiegen** (“to predominate” or
“to outweigh”): **überwiegt**, **überwog**, **hat überwogen**. Note that **überwiegend** is not the past participle;
that form is **überwogen**.

**Example:** *Die Rückmeldungen waren überwiegend positiv.* — “The feedback was mostly positive.”

Another example: *Die überwiegende Mehrheit der Gäste war zufrieden.* — “The vast majority of the guests were
satisfied.”

**English:** **mostly / predominantly / for the most part** · **French:** **majoritairement / principalement / en grande
partie**

Useful nuance: **Überwiegend** describes the larger share, not the whole: **überwiegend positive Rückmeldungen** allows
for some neutral or negative feedback. It is common in factual descriptions, reports, and everyday explanations, though
**meist** or **größtenteils** may sound more conversational. **Überwiegend** often concerns composition or proportion,
whereas **meistens** more often means “usually” across repeated occasions. The fixed expression **die überwiegende
Mehrheit** normally suggests a clear or substantial majority, rather than merely a narrow one.

## das Sprichwort

**CEFR:** roughly **B1**.

**Definition:** **Sprichwort** means **proverb**: a familiar, traditional saying that expresses a general observation,
piece of advice, or supposed truth about life. A typical example is **Übung macht den Meister** (“Practice makes
perfect”).

**Synonym:** **Redensart** (saying or idiomatic expression; related, but not necessarily a complete statement expressing
general wisdom); **Sinnspruch** (maxim or aphorism; often more literary and not necessarily traditional)

**Grammar:** **Sprichwort** is a neuter noun: **das Sprichwort**, genitive **des Sprichworts**, plural **die
Sprichwörter**. The plural has an umlaut; the dative plural is **den Sprichwörtern**. Common combinations include **ein
Sprichwort zitieren** (“to quote a proverb”), **ein bekanntes Sprichwort** (“a well-known proverb”), and **wie ein altes
Sprichwort sagt** (“as an old proverb says”). The related adjective is **sprichwörtlich** (“proverbial”).

**Example:** *Ein bekanntes Sprichwort lautet: „Übung macht den Meister.“* — “A well-known proverb says, ‘Practice makes
perfect.’”

Another example: *Meine Großmutter hat für jede Situation ein passendes Sprichwort.* — “My grandmother has a suitable
proverb for every situation.”

**English:** **proverb / saying** · **French:** **proverbe**

Useful nuance: A **Sprichwort** is typically a complete, fixed sentence that conveys general wisdom, such as **Viele
Köche verderben den Brei** (“Too many cooks spoil the broth”). A **Redewendung**, by contrast, is usually an idiomatic
phrase integrated into a sentence, such as **den Nagel auf den Kopf treffen** (“to hit the nail on the head”).
**Spruch** is a broader, often more informal term for a saying, slogan, or witty remark; not every **Spruch** is a
**Sprichwort**. Proverbs express traditional views, but their claims are not necessarily universally true.
