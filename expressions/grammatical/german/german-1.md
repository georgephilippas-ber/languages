## Sollen … doch + Infinitiv! (Soll er doch gehen!)

**CEFR:** roughly **B2–C1**.

**Definition:** A dismissive or defiant "let them…!": the speaker declares indifference to what someone else does, or
grudgingly leaves it to them, often with the undertone "I don't care, that's their problem". Short form: **Soll er
doch!** ("Let him!").

**Synonym:** **Von mir aus kann er … / Meinetwegen soll er … / Lass ihn doch …! / Dann soll er halt …**

**Grammar:** Verb-first exclamation: conjugated **sollen** in position 1, then the subject, the particle **doch**
(often with **ruhig**), any objects, and the **infinitive(s)** at the end. **sollen** agrees with the subject, which
is almost always third person: **Soll er / sie doch …!**, **Sollen die anderen doch …!** Several infinitives can share
one **sollen**: *Sollen sie doch streiten und verlieren!* A reflexive pronoun usually comes before a noun subject but
after a pronoun subject: *Sollen sich die anderen doch …* or *Sollen die anderen sich doch …*; *Soll er sich doch …*.
For the second person, use the imperative instead: *Dann geh doch!*

**Example:** *Sollen die anderen sich doch in den Wendungen und Verschlingungen des Gefechts verfangen und ihre
kleinen Siege genießen!* — "Let the others get caught up in the twists and tangles of the battle and enjoy their
little victories!"

Another example: *Wenn er unbedingt allein fahren will, soll er doch!* — "If he absolutely insists on going on his
own, let him!"

**English:** **Let them …! / Fine, let them …! / They can … for all I care.** · **French:** **Qu'ils … donc ! (+
subjonctif) / Libre à eux de … / Grand bien leur fasse !**

Useful nuance: word order and intonation decide the meaning. Verb-first with exclamatory stress, *Soll er doch morgen
kommen!*, is "Let him come tomorrow, then, I don't mind". Verb-second, *Er soll doch morgen kommen*, is a reminder or
reported expectation: "He's supposed to come tomorrow, isn't he?" Verb-first without **doch**, *Soll er morgen
kommen?*, is a plain question: "Should he come tomorrow?" Adding **ruhig** makes it genuine, relaxed permission
rather than defiance: *Soll er ruhig kommen* ("He's welcome to come"). French expresses the same attitude with
**que + subjonctif** (*Qu'il vienne, s'il veut !*).

## um … (Akkusativ) herum (um uns herum, um 8 Uhr herum)

**CEFR:** roughly **B1–B2**.

**Definition:** A two-part preposition (circumposition) that wraps around its noun: **um** before it, **herum** after
it. (1) In space, "around", in a circle round something or all around someone (*um den Tisch herum*, *die Welt um uns
herum*, "the world around us"). (2) With times, dates, and amounts, "around, roughly, about" (*um acht Uhr herum*,
*um Weihnachten herum*, *um die 50 Euro herum*). Adding **herum** to plain **um** stresses the surrounding or the
vagueness.

**Synonym:** **(1) um (alone) / rings um / rundherum / ringsherum (adverbs: all around)**; **(2) gegen / etwa /
ungefähr / circa / rund**

**Grammar:** **um** takes the **accusative**: *um den Platz herum*, *um uns herum*, *um die Stadt herum*. **herum**
comes after the noun phrase and closes it: *die Wälder **um** die Stadt **herum***. It can follow a noun as an
attribute (*die Welt um uns herum*) or work as an adverbial (*Wir fuhren um den See herum*). In speech **herum** is
often shortened to **rum** (*um die Ecke rum*). With times, plain **um** is exact (*um 8 Uhr* = "at 8") while **um …
herum** is approximate (*um 8 Uhr herum* = "around 8"); **gegen 8 Uhr** means the same. Don't confuse the
circumposition with separable verbs in **herum-**, where **herum** belongs to the verb: **herumlaufen** ("to walk
around, run about"), **um etwas herumkommen** ("to get around / avoid something": *Um eine Reform kommen wir nicht
herum*), **herumreden um + Akkusativ** ("to beat about the bush").

**Example:** *Als wir aufrecht gingen, konnten wir mehr von der Welt um uns herum sehen.* — "When we walked upright,
we could see more of the world around us."

Another example: *Ich bin um halb sieben herum zu Hause, vielleicht etwas später.* — "I'll be home around half past
six, maybe a bit later."

**English:** **around / round (space); around / about / roughly (time, amounts)**; *die Welt um uns herum* = "the
world around us" · **French:** **autour de (space); vers / aux alentours de / environ (time, amounts)**; *le monde
autour de nous*

Useful nuance: German has several of these two-part prepositions: **um … herum** ("around"), **von … an / ab** ("from
… on": *von heute an*), **von … aus** ("from": *vom Fenster aus*), **an … entlang** ("along": *an der Mauer entlang*),
**um … willen** ("for the sake of": *um des Friedens willen*). The second part is not optional decoration: *die Welt
um uns* sounds clipped and poetic, while *die Welt um uns herum* is the natural everyday phrase. In the example
sentence, **konnten** is in second position after the **als**-clause (the whole clause counts as position 1), and the
infinitive **sehen** goes to the end, as with every modal verb.

## jemandem / einer Sache (Dativ) nahe sein / näher sein als / am nächsten sein

**CEFR:** roughly **B1–B2**.

**Definition:** To be close to someone or something: in space (*Das Hotel ist dem Bahnhof sehr nahe*), in time (*Wir
sind dem Ziel nahe*), in feeling or relationship (*Sie ist ihrer Schwester sehr nahe*), or in understanding and
experience (*Die alten Griechen waren dem Übergang … näher als wir*, "closer to the transition than we are"). The
person or thing one is close to goes in the **dative**, with no preposition.

**Synonym:** **in der Nähe von + Dativ sein (space) / nahestehen + Dativ (emotionally) / kurz vor etwas (Dativ)
stehen (time: *kurz vor dem Ziel*)**; **antonym: fern sein + Dativ (elevated) / weit entfernt sein von + Dativ**

**Grammar:** Pattern: **Subjekt + sein + Dativ + nahe / näher / am nächsten**. The comparison is irregular: **nah(e)
– näher – am nächsten / der nächste**. The dative usually comes before the adjective: *Er war **dem Tod** nahe*
("He was close to death"); *Wir sind **der Lösung** näher als gestern*. The comparison follows **als**: *näher als
wir*. With a pronoun: *Er ist **mir** sehr nahe gekommen*. Fixed phrases with the dative: **den Tränen nahe sein**
("to be close to tears"), **dem Wahnsinn nahe sein** ("to be close to madness"), **der Wahrheit näher kommen** ("to
get closer to the truth"), **jemandem zu nahe treten** ("to offend someone, overstep the mark"). Related verbs,
written together: **nahestehen + Dativ** ("to be close to", emotionally or politically: *eine der Regierung
nahestehende Zeitung*), **nahekommen + Dativ** ("to come close to, approximate": *Das kommt der Wahrheit nahe*),
**nahelegen** ("to suggest": *jemandem etwas nahelegen*), **naheliegen** ("to be obvious": *Die Vermutung liegt
nahe*).

**Example:** *Die alten Griechen waren dem Übergang des Menschen vom Animalischen zum Rationalen näher als wir.* —
"The ancient Greeks were closer than we are to humanity's transition from the animal to the rational."

Another example: *Nach der dritten Absage war sie den Tränen nahe.* — "After the third rejection, she was close to
tears."

**English:** **to be close to / near (to) / closer to / closest to; to be on the verge of (*den Tränen nahe*)** ·
**French:** **être proche de / près de / plus proche de; être au bord de (*au bord des larmes*)**

Useful nuance: English and French use a preposition ("close **to**", "proche **de**"), so learners tend to add one in
German: ~~*nahe zu dem Ziel*~~. The bare dative is the standard pattern; **nahe an + Dativ** is used only for physical
closeness (*nahe am Fenster*), and **in der Nähe von** is the everyday spatial alternative (*in der Nähe vom
Bahnhof*). The same "adjective + dative" pattern appears with other adjectives: **jemandem ähnlich** ("similar to"),
**dankbar** ("grateful to"), **treu** ("loyal to"), **fremd** ("strange to"), **bekannt** ("known to"), **egal**
("all the same to": *Das ist mir egal*).

## das + substantiviertes Adjektiv (das Animalische, das Rationale, das Gute)

**CEFR:** roughly **B2–C1**.

**Definition:** Turning an adjective into an abstract noun by adding the neuter article and a capital letter, to
name a quality or a category of things as an idea: **das Animalische** ("the animal (side), what is animal"), **das
Rationale** ("the rational"), **das Gute / das Böse** ("good / evil"), **das Schöne** ("beauty, the beautiful"),
**das Neue** ("what is new"). Common in philosophy, essays, and art criticism, and in everyday phrases like **das
Beste**, **alles Gute**, **etwas Neues**, **nichts Besonderes**.

**Synonym:** **an abstract noun where one exists: die Rationalität / die Vernunft (das Rationale), die Tierhaftigkeit
/ die Triebe (das Animalische), die Schönheit (das Schöne)** — usually more concrete and less philosophical

**Grammar:** Capitalise the adjective and decline it like an adjective after the article (weak declension after
**das / dem / des**): **das Animalische, des Animalischen, dem Animalischen**. After contracted prepositions the
dative **-en** appears: **vom Animalischen zum Rationalen** (*von dem*, *zu dem*). After **etwas, nichts, viel,
wenig, allerlei** it takes strong neuter endings: **etwas Neues, nichts Gutes, viel Interessantes**; after **alles**
weak ones: **alles Gute**. No plural in the abstract sense. Masculine and feminine forms name people instead: **der
Fremde / die Fremde** ("the stranger"), **die Alten** ("the old"), **die Reichen** ("the rich"); plural **die alten
Griechen** is ordinary adjective + noun.

**Example:** *Die alten Griechen waren dem Übergang des Menschen vom Animalischen zum Rationalen näher als wir.* —
"The ancient Greeks were closer than we are to humanity's transition from the animal to the rational."

Another example: *Er sucht in jeder Situation das Positive, selbst wenn es schwierig ist.* — "He looks for the
positive in every situation, even when it's difficult."

**English:** **the + adjective (the rational, the beautiful) / what is + adjective / the + adjective + side (the
animal side) / -ness nouns (goodness)** · **French:** **le + adjectif (le rationnel, le beau, l'animal → l'animalité)
/ ce qui est + adjectif**

Useful nuance: English can say "the rational" or "the beautiful" only in a few philosophical set phrases; German does
it freely with almost any adjective, so translations often need "what is …", "the … side", or a "-ness" noun:
**das Unheimliche** ("the uncanny"), **das Wesentliche** ("the essentials"), **das Typische daran** ("what's typical
about it"). Don't forget the capital letter: *im Allgemeinen*, *das Gleiche*, *im Großen und Ganzen*. The ending
changes with the article and case like any adjective, so **vom Rationalen** (dative), but **das Rationale**
(nominative/accusative).

## derjenige / diejenige / dasjenige / diejenigen, der / die / das … (+ Relativsatz)

**CEFR:** roughly **B2**.

**Definition:** A demonstrative pronoun meaning "the one(s) (who / which)", "those (who)", "that (which)". It points
forward to a relative clause that says which person or thing is meant: **diejenigen, die …** = "those who …"
(*Diejenigen, die früh buchen, bekommen Rabatt*). It can also stand before a noun as a determiner: **diejenigen
Teilnehmer, die …** ("those participants who …"). It is somewhat formal and emphatic.

**Synonym:** **der / die / das (stressed demonstrative: *Die, die früh buchen, …*) / wer (generalising: *Wer früh
bucht, …*) / alle, die … (everyone who) / jene, die … (elevated)**

**Grammar:** It is one word made of two declined parts: the article part **der-** and the adjective part **-jenige**,
which takes weak adjective endings. Both change for gender, number, and case:

* **Nominativ:** derjenige · diejenige · dasjenige · diejenigen
* **Akkusativ:** denjenigen · diejenige · dasjenige · diejenigen
* **Dativ:** demjenigen · derjenigen · demjenigen · denjenigen
* **Genitiv:** desjenigen · derjenigen · desjenigen · derjenigen

The pronoun takes its case from its role in the main clause; the relative pronoun takes its case from its role in
the relative clause: *Ich danke **denjenigen** (Dativ, *danken*), **die** (Nominativ) mitgeholfen haben*;
*Diejenigen, **denen** (Dativ, *helfen*) wir geholfen haben, …*. A comma always separates the relative clause. Never
contract the article part with a preposition: **von demjenigen**, not ~~*vom jenigen*~~.

**Example:** *Diejenigen, die sich rechtzeitig anmelden, erhalten einen Platz in der ersten Reihe.* — "Those who
register in time will get a seat in the front row."

Another example: *Der Preis geht an denjenigen, dessen Entwurf die Jury am meisten überzeugt.* — "The prize goes to
the one whose design the jury finds most convincing."

**English:** **the one(s) who / which; those who; that which / the one** · **French:** **celui / celle / ceux /
celles qui (que, dont)** (*Ceux qui s'inscrivent à temps …*)

Useful nuance: **diejenigen, die …** is formal and slightly heavy; everyday German often uses the stressed
demonstrative **die, die …** or a **wer**-clause instead: *Wer zu spät kommt, muss draußen warten* (rather than
*Diejenigen, die zu spät kommen, …*). Use **derjenige** when you want to single out precisely which one, or in
writing to avoid the clumsy-looking **der, der**. Don't confuse it with **derselbe** ("the same one"), which is also
written as one word and declined in both parts (*am selben Tag*, *mit demselben Auto*). Unlike French *celui-ci /
celui-là*, **derjenige** can't stand alone: it needs a following relative clause or noun.
